Papercup offered dubbing for media companies. Its system translated the dialogue of a video and generated speech in another language that matched the tone and timing of the original, with human translators and editors checking the results. Publishers, broadcasters, and content owners used it to put news, documentaries, and entertainment in front of audiences in new markets without paying for traditional dubbing studios.

The approach mixed machine speed with human oversight. Linguists reviewed translations, adjusted phrasing, and corrected pronunciation, which kept quality higher than fully automatic dubbing and made the service suitable for professional content. It also let customers keep control over the voice style and brand.

The business ended when RWS, a language and content technology company, bought the intellectual property behind the dubbing technology, and Papercup stopped operating as an independent service. Customers needed to move to other providers. Alternatives include ElevenLabs Dubbing, Deepdub for film and television, HeyGen's video translation, Rask, and Dubverse, along with traditional dubbing studios. This entry stays for reference, because older coverage of AI dubbing often cites Papercup as a pioneer.
