For research, Perplexity is useful because it shows its sources. Each answer comes with numbered citations to the pages it used, so you can open the link and check a claim before relying on it. You can narrow searches to particular kinds of sources, such as academic papers, and keep asking follow-up questions that build on the earlier ones. This makes it a quicker starting point than a list of search results for questions that need several sources.

For larger questions, Deep Research runs many searches, reads the pages, and compiles a structured report in a few minutes. Spaces collect related searches, uploaded files, and custom instructions in a single project, so you can return to a topic and share it with a team. You can upload PDFs and other documents and ask questions about them, and the Pro plans let you choose among several underlying models.

The free plan covers basic searches, and paid plans raise the limits for deep research and advanced models. Treat it as a fast first pass: it can still misread a source, rely on a weak one, or miss material that is not freely available online. Academic work should go back to the original papers. Consensus and Elicit are more specialized for scientific literature, and ChatGPT and Gemini also offer deep research modes. Perplexity is strongest for current, web-based questions.
