Trello organizes work on a board. Lists represent stages like To Do, Doing, and Done, and each task is a card that you drag from list to list. Cards can hold descriptions, checklists, due dates, attachments, comments, and assigned members. The simplicity is the point: most people learn it in minutes, which makes it popular for small teams, personal projects, content calendars, and simple workflows like hiring or event planning.

Beyond the basic board, Trello adds views, automation, and power-ups. Calendar, timeline, table, and dashboard views show the same cards in different ways. Built-in automation, called Butler, runs rules and buttons, like moving a card when a checklist is complete or assigning someone when a card enters a list. Power-Ups connect to tools such as Slack, Google Drive, and Jira. Atlassian's AI features can summarize a card's activity and help turn notes or messages into cards.

Trello has a free plan with generous basics, and paid plans add more views, automation runs, and admin features. It belongs to Atlassian, so it connects with Jira and Confluence. Its simplicity becomes a limit on large projects with dependencies, resource planning, or detailed reporting, where Asana, Jira, or ClickUp serve better. Notion and Monday.com overlap for flexible team use. For lightweight, visual task tracking it is still one of the easiest choices.
