Recursion approaches drug discovery as a data problem. Its labs run enormous numbers of experiments in which cells are treated with genetic changes or compounds and photographed under microscopes. Machine learning analyzes the images to find how diseases change cell appearance and which compounds reverse those changes, building a large map of biology that the company calls the Recursion OS. The idea is to find promising targets and molecules faster than with traditional trial-and-error screening.

The merger with Exscientia added strength in chemistry, with AI-driven design and automated synthesis of small molecules. The combined company has its own pipeline of candidate drugs in areas such as oncology and rare diseases, and works with large pharmaceutical partners through collaborations that can bring milestone payments. It also contributes models and datasets to the scientific community, and works with computing partners on foundation models for biology.

Recursion is a public company, not a product you can buy, and its results depend on clinical trials, which are slow and often fail whatever the quality of the discovery process. The question of whether AI-driven discovery produces better drugs than conventional approaches is still being tested. Peers include Isomorphic Labs, Insilico Medicine, Schrödinger, and Absci, along with large pharmaceutical companies building their own AI groups. It's a notable example of a lab-heavy approach to AI drug discovery.
