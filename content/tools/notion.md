Notion works as a set of building blocks. A team can create a company wiki, a project tracker with a board view, a product roadmap, a meeting notes database, and a documentation site, all in one workspace with permissions and comments. Databases are central: the same set of records can be viewed as a table, board, calendar, or timeline, and linked to other databases, which makes it possible to build custom systems without code.

Recent additions have widened its scope. Notion Calendar and Notion Mail connect scheduling and email to the workspace, and Notion Sites publishes pages as websites. Notion AI answers questions across the workspace and connected apps, drafts and edits content, takes meeting notes, and runs agents that automate tasks, all subject to plan and credit limits. Integrations link Notion with Slack, GitHub, Google Drive, and more.

Notion has a free plan for individuals and small teams, and paid plans that add collaboration, permissions, and AI. Its flexibility is a strength and a trap: workspaces can become messy without conventions, and large databases may be slow. Alternatives include Confluence, Coda, Airtable, ClickUp, and Obsidian for personal notes. See the other Notion entries for notes-focused and AI-focused views. Notion suits teams that want one adaptable workspace.
