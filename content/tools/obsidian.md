Obsidian treats notes as local files. Everything you write is a Markdown document in a folder on your computer, so you own the data and can open it with any other editor. You connect notes with links, and the app shows backlinks and a graph view of how ideas relate. People use it for personal knowledge management, research, writing, journaling, and project notes, and many build large networks of thousands of linked notes over years.

Its flexibility comes from plugins. Community plugins add calendars, task management, spaced repetition, diagrams, databases, and much more, and themes change how it looks. AI plugins can summarize notes, answer questions about your vault using semantic search, and generate text with models from OpenAI, Anthropic, or local options like Ollama. Because the notes are plain files, they work well with AI tools and scripts that read the folder directly.

Obsidian is free for personal use, and paid add-ons offer Sync across devices and Publish for putting notes on the web, with a commercial license for businesses. There is no cloud collaboration like Notion offers, so it fits solo use better than team wikis. The learning curve is steeper because so much is configurable. Logseq, Notion, Roam Research, and Apple Notes are the common alternatives, with Logseq the closest open-source option.
