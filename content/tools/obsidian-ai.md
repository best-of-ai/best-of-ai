Obsidian's approach to AI follows its general philosophy: keep the core simple and let users choose add-ons. Because notes are plain files in a folder, they work well with language models. Community plugins let you chat with your vault, ask questions about your notes, summarize long documents, rewrite text, and find related notes by meaning instead of keywords. Smart Connections and Copilot-style plugins are popular examples.

Users have a lot of freedom over where the AI runs. A plugin can send text to a hosted model like those from OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google using your own API key, or it can connect to a local model through tools such as Ollama or LM Studio, so notes never leave your computer. Some people also expose their vault to coding agents and assistants through the Model Context Protocol, which lets tools like Claude read and organize notes.

The plugins are free or low-cost, but you pay for model usage if you use a hosted service. Setup takes more effort than turning on a feature in Notion, and quality varies by plugin and model. Privacy depends on your configuration, so check what each plugin sends. Notion AI and Capacities have built-in assistants, Logseq has its own plugins, and NotebookLM offers a polished interface for source-grounded questions. Obsidian suits people who value local control.
