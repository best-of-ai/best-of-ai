WeatherBug built its name on a dense network of weather stations, often at schools and other local sites. That gives it temperature, wind, and rain readings from places closer to you than a distant airport sensor, which helps when conditions change quickly, such as during a storm. The app combines that observation data with forecast models for hourly and multi-day outlooks.

Its mobile app is the main product. Users get radar maps, lightning detection, and alerts for severe weather and rainfall, along with air quality and pollen information in some regions. A subscription removes ads and unlocks extra features, and some of the forecasting uses machine learning to adjust model output for local conditions.

WeatherBug has changed owners over the years, and the consumer app is now separate from the company's earlier business-focused weather products. Like many free weather apps, it is supported by advertising and location data, so read the privacy settings. Alternatives include AccuWeather, The Weather Channel, Carrot Weather, Apple Weather, and Weather Underground. WeatherBug suits people who want local conditions and storm alerts.
