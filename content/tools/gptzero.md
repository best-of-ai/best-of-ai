GPTZero was created by a Princeton student soon after ChatGPT appeared and became one of the most used AI detectors. You paste text or upload a document, and it returns the probability that the writing is human or AI, with sentences highlighted by likelihood. It looks at features such as perplexity and burstiness, which describe how predictable and varied the writing is, and uses trained models for classification. Teachers, editors, and publishers use it to flag content for a closer look.

The product has expanded to address the weaknesses of detection alone. Tools can track the writing process, showing how a document was composed over time, so that a student can demonstrate they wrote it. A Chrome extension and integrations with learning management systems like Canvas help educators, and additional features check for plagiarism and fabricated citations.

GPTZero has a free tier with limits and paid plans for more words and features. Reliability is the core concern: AI detectors produce false positives, especially for non-native English writers and formal prose, and they can be fooled by paraphrasing. A score should start a conversation and never serve as proof of cheating. Turnitin, Copyleaks, and Originality.ai are competitors, and many schools are shifting toward process-based assessment and clearer AI policies.
