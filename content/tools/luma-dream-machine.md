Dream Machine is how most people first used Luma AI's video models. You describe a scene or upload a still image, and it produces a short clip with motion, lighting, and camera movement that tends to look cinematic. Luma started as a 3D capture company, which still shows in how well its video models handle depth and camera moves. People use it for concept videos, storyboards, social content, product shots, and b-roll when a shoot is not practical.

Control over the output has become the main focus. Keyframes let you set a start and end image, so the clip has to travel between them. Modify tools restyle or change elements in existing video while keeping the original motion, and reframe options adapt a clip to a different aspect ratio. Luma's Ray models are the engine behind these tools, and newer versions of the app add agents that plan and run multi-step creative jobs across text, images, video, and audio. The branding has shifted over time, and Dream Machine is now often just called Luma.

Access is through the web and mobile apps. There is a free tier with limited generations, and paid plans give more credits, higher resolution, and commercial use. As with other video generators, results vary from prompt to prompt, so you should expect to generate several takes and pick the best. Clip length is short, and longer pieces have to be assembled in an editor. Main competitors include Runway, Google's Veo, Kling, and Pika.
