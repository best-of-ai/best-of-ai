Schrödinger builds software that simulates molecules. Its approach relies on physics: calculating how atoms interact to predict properties like how strongly a drug candidate binds to a protein. The best-known method, free energy perturbation, estimates binding strength with accuracy useful enough to guide which molecules to make in the lab. Pharmaceutical and biotech companies use the platform to design and screen compounds before synthesizing them.

Machine learning is combined with these simulations. Models trained on simulation results can score vast numbers of molecules rapidly, which makes it possible to explore chemical space far beyond what physical calculations alone could cover. The software suite includes Maestro as the main interface and LiveDesign for collaboration among scientists, with applications in materials science, such as batteries and polymers, alongside life sciences.

Schrödinger is a public company with a software business sold by license, mainly to enterprise customers and academia at lower cost, and a drug discovery arm with its own and partnered programs. Simulations need expert users and substantial computing power, and predictions still need experimental validation. Competitors and peers include Cresset, OpenEye (part of Cadence), Dassault Systèmes BIOVIA, and AI-first companies like Isomorphic Labs and Recursion. It's a leader in physics-based computational design.
