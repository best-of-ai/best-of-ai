Zapier connects the apps a business already uses and lets them pass data to each other without code. You build a Zap with a trigger, such as a new form submission, and one or more actions, such as adding a row to a spreadsheet, posting in Slack, and creating a CRM contact. It supports thousands of apps, from Gmail and Salesforce to niche tools, which is the main reason people choose it over building integrations by hand. Filters, paths, and delays let a Zap branch and wait, and a multi-step workflow can handle surprisingly complicated processes.

The AI side has grown quickly. Copilot builds and edits Zaps from a plain-language description, so you can say what you want automated and review what it creates. Zapier Agents are AI assistants that can research, draft, and take actions across your connected apps toward a goal you give them. Zapier MCP lets an AI client like Claude or Cursor use Zapier's app connections, which turns a chat assistant into something that can act in your tools. Tables and Interfaces add a small database and simple web forms and pages, and AI steps can be dropped into any Zap to summarize, classify, or extract information.

Zapier has a free plan with a small number of tasks, and paid plans scale by how many tasks you run each month. Usage-based pricing can climb quickly for high-volume workflows, which is the usual reason teams move to cheaper or self-hosted tools such as Make or n8n. Zapier is easier for non-developers to start with, and the app coverage is hard to beat. It works best for moderate-volume business workflows where saving setup time matters more than saving cost per task.
