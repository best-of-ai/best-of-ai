Cartesia focuses on speed in voice AI. Its Sonic models, built on state-space model architectures instead of only transformers, generate natural-sounding speech with very low latency, so a voice agent can start speaking almost as soon as a response is ready. That matters for phone calls and live assistants, where delays feel awkward. The company also offers voice cloning and control over emotion and speaking rate.

The platform has grown into a set of real-time voice building blocks. Sonic handles text-to-speech, Ink handles speech-to-text, and tools for managed voice agents help companies deploy agents that answer phone calls. Developers connect through APIs and SDKs, and Cartesia works with common voice agent frameworks and telephony providers. Multilingual support lets one system serve customers in different languages.

Cartesia has a free tier for testing and pay-as-you-go pricing for production, plus enterprise arrangements. It is aimed at developers and companies building voice products, not at creators looking for a studio to narrate videos, where ElevenLabs or Murf are more convenient. Competitors for real-time voice include ElevenLabs, Deepgram, OpenAI's voice models, and Rime. Voice quality and latency vary with language and network conditions, so testing with your own use case is wise.
