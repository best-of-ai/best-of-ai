Cronometer is for people who want to know what is in their food, not only how much of it they eat. You log meals by searching a database, scanning barcodes, or entering recipes, and the app breaks them down into calories, macronutrients, and dozens of micronutrients. Its data comes from verified sources such as government nutrition databases, which makes it more reliable than apps built mostly on user-submitted entries.

Detailed tracking supports use cases like managing a medical diet, checking for vitamin and mineral gaps, tracking fasting or specific eating plans, and training for sports. Targets can be customized, charts show trends, and the app can connect with fitness trackers and devices to account for activity. Newer features use AI to speed up logging, for example by recognizing foods from photos, though human review is wise.

Cronometer has a free tier and a paid Gold plan with more features, such as extra analysis and no ads. Entering everything takes discipline, and accurate tracking still depends on how well you estimate portions. Nutrition tracking is not suitable for everyone, particularly people with a history of disordered eating, who should talk to a professional first. MyFitnessPal has the largest database, Lose It is simple for weight loss, and MacroFactor focuses on adaptive calorie targets.
