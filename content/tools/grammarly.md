Grammarly started as a grammar checker and became the writing helper people keep installed everywhere. A browser extension, desktop app, mobile keyboard, and plugins for Word and Google Docs underline mistakes in emails, messages, and documents as you type. It catches typos and grammar errors, flags wordy or unclear sentences, and suggests a different tone, such as more confident or more friendly. A free version covers the basics.

Generative AI is built into the same flow. You can ask it to rewrite a paragraph, shorten a message, draft a reply to an email, or brainstorm ideas from a prompt. AI agents focus on particular jobs, such as giving feedback on a student paper, predicting how a reader might respond, or checking citations. Paid tiers add full-sentence rewrites, tone detection, plagiarism checking, and an AI detector, and business plans add style guides, brand tone, and analytics for teams.

The company behind Grammarly has taken the name Superhuman and also owns the Superhuman email app and the Coda workspace, with the writing product still sold as Grammarly. Because it reads what you type in many apps, privacy deserves a look: Grammarly states that it does not sell your data, and business plans have stricter controls. Its suggestions are often right and sometimes wrong, particularly for creative writing and specialized terminology, so accept them with judgment. ProWritingAid and LanguageTool are alternatives, and ChatGPT or Claude can do deeper rewriting, though without the always-on integration that makes Grammarly convenient.
